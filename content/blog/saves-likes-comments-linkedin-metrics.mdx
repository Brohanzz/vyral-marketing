# Saves vs Likes vs Comments: Which LinkedIn Metric Actually Predicts Growth

Most creators default to likes as the metric that matters, since it is the most visible number on every post. It is also the weakest signal of the three for understanding what is actually building your audience over time.

## What Each Metric Actually Signals

**Likes** are the lowest-effort engagement action available. A like takes a fraction of a second and requires almost no commitment from the reader. This makes likes useful as a rough indicator of reach and surface-level resonance, but a weak indicator of how much value the content actually delivered.

**Comments** require active effort: the reader has to form a thought and type it out. Comments signal stronger engagement than likes, and LinkedIn's own platform behavior treats comments as a meaningfully stronger distribution signal than likes, since comments indicate a post sparked enough reaction to warrant a response.

**Saves** are the most deliberate of the three. Saving a post means the reader intends to return to it later, which is a strong signal that the content has standalone reference value, not just momentary interest. A save is functionally a reader saying "this is useful enough that I want it again."

## Why Saves Are the Underrated Metric

Likes are abundant and easy to inflate through network reciprocity (people liking your posts because you like theirs). Comments can also be influenced by engagement pods or reciprocal commenting circles, which is part of why LinkedIn has cracked down on automated engagement schemes. Saves are much harder to manufacture artificially, since there is no reciprocal social pressure to save someone else's post the way there is to like or comment on it.

This makes saves a cleaner signal of genuine content value. A post with a high save rate relative to its impressions is producing something readers consider worth referencing again, which is a stronger indicator of audience trust and authority-building than raw like counts.

## What This Means for Content Strategy

**Educational and reference-style content tends to drive saves.** Frameworks, step-by-step breakdowns, and data-backed insights are the content types readers are most likely to want to revisit, which is part of why this format often builds long-term authority more effectively than purely entertaining or emotionally resonant content, even when the latter drives more likes.

**Comments are worth actively inviting, not just hoping for.** Posts that end with a genuine question, or take a clear enough position that readers want to weigh in, tend to generate comments more reliably than posts that simply state information.

**Do not optimize purely for likes.** A post with high likes but low saves and comments may be reaching people without building the kind of trust that compounds into long-term growth.

## A Practical Way to Read Your Own Metrics

Rather than tracking each metric in isolation, look at the relationship between them on your best-performing posts. A post with disproportionately high saves relative to likes is signaling strong reference value. A post with disproportionately high comments relative to impressions is signaling that it sparked genuine discussion. Both are stronger growth indicators than likes alone, even when likes is the larger raw number.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which LinkedIn metric matters most for growth?

No single metric tells the full story, but saves and comments are generally stronger indicators of genuine audience value than likes, since both require more effort from the reader and are harder to inflate artificially.

### Why do my posts get likes but not comments or saves?

This often indicates the content is reaching people and generating surface-level approval, but is not delivering enough standalone value or a strong enough point of view to prompt deeper engagement.

### Can engagement metrics be artificially inflated?

Likes and comments can be influenced by reciprocal engagement circles, which is part of why LinkedIn has taken enforcement action against automation tools that facilitate this. Saves are comparatively harder to manufacture, since there is no social reciprocity pressure attached to saving content.

### Should I stop tracking likes entirely?

No. Likes still provide a useful, if weaker, signal of reach and initial resonance. The recommendation is to weigh saves and comments more heavily when evaluating which content is genuinely building your authority and audience.
